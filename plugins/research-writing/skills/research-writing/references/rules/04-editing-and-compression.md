# Edit without losing the argument

Use these rules after the contribution and dependency inventories exist.
Cuts must preserve the reasons, evidence and scientific scope that make the account defensible.
The examples below are synthetic teaching examples.

## X01. Cut empty requirements and significance claims

Ask what a sentence adds here: a result, reason, definition, consequence or concrete open question.
A review instruction belongs in the working record when it contributes no scientific content to the passage.

**Synthetic example:** “A fair comparison must count every cost” names no experiment or consequence.
Replace it with the actual cost convention when that convention matters, or remove it from the manuscript.
“Readout time is excluded from this estimate” identifies an interpretive limit when the estimate concerns total cost.
Retain the limit wherever the claim needs it.

Keep precise limitations such as an unmeasured quantity or an input condition that changes the result.
Delete a redundant instruction to validate a failure when the failure itself already states the scientific point.
Use the [worked examples](../cases/01-revision-lessons.md) to distinguish scientific scope from review narration.

**Check:** Does the sentence change the reader's understanding of the research, or does it only describe the review process?

## X02. Name useful future work

State the completed result first.
Name an unresolved task when it informs interpretation or the research programme.
Give the task an object, action and reason.
A wider hardware study can test a noise limit.
An extension to a specified loss can test whether the result survives a changed objective.

Use concrete future work in a discussion when it follows from the evidence.
An abstract can end with the contribution when another qualification would repeat an already clear limit.
Avoid appending a future-work sentence to every paragraph.

**Check:** Can readers identify what the proposed study would test and why that answer matters?
Cut a second closing sentence when it adds no new information.

## X03. Put detail where the reader needs it

| Location | Main job | Detail to retain |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | Motivate the field, challenge and contribution. | Clear results and essential scope. |
| Introduction | Explain the research choice. | Evidence for the choice and why the contribution matters. |
| Background | Supply reusable concepts. | Definitions and results that later sections need. |
| Methods | Explain and specify the construction. | Mechanism, assumptions and enough detail to understand it. |
| Results | State findings and interpretation. | Comparisons and qualifications needed to read the evidence. |
| Appendix | Supply supporting arguments and settings. | Full proofs and reproducibility details. |

Before deleting technical material, check incoming references and later uses.
Record a destination for each retained dependency.
A lengthy derivation can move when its result and necessary conditions remain available.
Keep a connection between gate families or readout support when later claims depend on it.
A derivation with no later use can be a better compression target.

Use the source's main contributions and figures to guide the choice.
Move long proofs, repeated background and configuration lists before cutting the construction or its main evidence.
Retain established method names, such as “data reuploading”, when they identify the construction precisely.
Respect the venue's requirements for methods and supplementary material.

**Check:** Can readers still understand, assess and reproduce the contribution at the required level?

## X04. Remove repeated jobs, not every repeated word

A roadmap, summary and significance sentence can perform the same job.
Keep the version that helps readers at that location.
Check nearby paragraphs before adding another explanation of why a topic matters.

A brief recap can support an independent reading of a contribution chapter.
A conclusion can restate the research questions to answer them explicitly.
Resolve these cases through the passage's role and the author's decision.
Repeated terminology can preserve meaning when synonyms would blur the object.

**Check:** Identify the reading need served by each repetition before removing it.

## X05. Preserve distinctions that change meaning

Keep the distinction between a theorem and a finite-range observation.
Keep the distinction between a simulated prediction and a measured quantity.
State each directly and remove rhetorical framing that adds no information.

Compression must preserve assumptions, uncertainty, counterexamples and experimental identity.
A short sentence that hides one of these facts fails the scientific review.
Recheck captions, summaries and conclusions when compression changes a result sentence.

**Check:** Compare the revised passage with its claim record and source contribution.
Every condition that changes the meaning needs an appropriate destination.

## L01. Use a clear actor and a precise verb

Use the manuscript's accepted narrative voice for the contribution.
Use “we”, “our” and “us” when the author selects that voice.
Prefer actions such as construct, compare, estimate and measure.
A vague statement about what a framework makes clear can hide the actual operation.
Classify the claim before choosing its verb.

**Check:** Can readers identify the actor, action and object?

## L02. Avoid formulaic turns of phrase

Follow the [author style](../author-style.md) and [writing standard](../writing-standard.md).
State the actual claim directly.
Avoid corrective juxtaposition, stock significance language and generic announcements about the next passage.
Replace vague antecedents and unnecessary status words with the object or action they describe.

Keep established scientific terms when a substitute would obscure the meaning.
Preserve exact external titles and necessary attributed terminology.
Source quotations used as review evidence can retain a rejected pattern when their exact wording matters.
Label them as evidence and keep their provenance in the working record.

**Check:** Does the phrase carry a scientific distinction, or does it only give the sentence a familiar rhetorical shape?

## L03. Use style metrics as prompts for reading

Sentence length, repeated openings and keyword matches can locate passages for review.
They cannot establish clarity, truth or author voice.
Short sentences can still lose the reasons that connect an argument.

Use the current [writing standard](../writing-standard.md).
Aim for one idea per sentence, with procedural sentences of at most 20 words and descriptive sentences of at most 25.
Use active voice, familiar words and a consistent term for each object.
Keep necessary articles and technically precise language.
Vary neighbouring sentence length and structure where the reasoning allows it.
A necessary clear explanation takes precedence over a mechanical count.

Read a large cut aloud before accepting it.
Compare the reasons in the revision with the original account.
Use the author's accepted passages to assess voice in context.

**Check:** Can a reader follow the reasoning without the conversation or the style report?
